How to read this document

  • Direct A source shows the property changes how people understand an AI explanation, a probability or forecast, or a warning.
  • Indirect A source shows it affects comprehension in general text or interfaces, or in people with cognitive impairments. Applying it to explanations is our inference.
  • None found Marked as possibly relevant, but no source links it to understanding. Kept as our inference.
  • B · XAI changes what is explained or how it is understood. C · Both a UI guideline that also shapes the explanation.
  • Research Approach v2 from the original triage. New, manual marking added from the colour-coded catalogue. A red note shows where the catalogue marking differs.
  • Each source says how it was checked: full text or abstract. Nothing rests on a search snippet. "Counter" marks evidence against the guideline.

Limits

No source tests these guidelines on AI explanations for people with dementia, MCI, autism or ADHD, so every link to that population is our inference. None of the 49 catalogue sources discusses AI explanations. Publisher sites were blocked during the search, so most sources were checked at abstract level.

Why standard XAI output needs this adaptation at all

  • Miller (2019), Artificial Intelligence 267. It is "not reasonable to expect a lay-user to be able to interpret a causal chain"; explanations are social and fitted to what the listener knows. full text
  • Liao, Gruen & S. Miller (2020), CHI. Lay users ask for explanations; practitioners found LIME feature-importance lists "unsatisfying". full text
  • Schuff et al. (2022), FAccT. Lay users "often mis-interpret" feature-attribution explanations, including Shapley-based ones. full text
  • Tielman et al. (2024), Technology in Society 79. "Adaptive XAI is the key to inclusive XAI"; it calls for personalisation, "more than just simplicity in text". full text

G14
Use clear words
B · XAINew, manual marking
 Raw XAI output uses feature names (soil_moisture_72h, "probability"). Each reason must become everyday words ("the ground is already wet"). A lookup table in the data model maps each model input to plain words in Sinhala, Tamil and English. The "Why?" screen reads only from it.

Bullock et al. (2019), Public Underst. Sci. Jargon "impairs people's ability to process scientific information" (N=650). abstract

Walpole et al. (2025), Risk Analysis. "Evacuation warning" was poorly understood in emergency alerts; omit jargon where possible. abstract

Zytek et al. (2022). "ML models are only as interpretable as their features"; features must be readable by users. full text

Gigerenzer et al. (2005), Risk Analysis. "30% chance of rain" is read in contradictory ways unless the reference class is stated. abstract
G16
Avoid double negatives or nested clauses
B · XAINew, manual marking
 Uncertainty wording invites negatives ("it is not unlikely"). One clause per sentence; no negated likelihood words. A banned-phrase list in the template rules; Layer 4 AI wording that contains one is rejected.

Teigen & Brun (1995), Acta Psychologica. "Unlikely" and "doubtful" "function more like negations". abstract

Teigen & Brun (1999), OBHDP. Phrases of equal probability lead to different decisions depending on direction. abstract

Budescu et al. (2009), Psychological Science. Readers' numeric readings of IPCC probability words "deviated significantly" from the guidelines. abstract

Xiang et al. (2020): negative sentences cost more processing. Nasiri et al. (2022): mild Alzheimer's impairs relative-clause sentences. abstract

"Not unlikely"-style wording itself is untested; that step is our inference.
G18
Keep text succinct
B · XAINew, manual marking
 Each explanation step is one sentence of 15 words or fewer; the five invariants fit in three sentences. A word budget per text slot in each screen spec, so the forecast card never scrolls. mixed evidence

Narayanan et al. (2018), preprint. Longer, more complex ML explanations raised response time and lowered satisfaction; accuracy stayed stable. summary

van Boxtel et al. (2021). Comprehension of long sentences declines with ageing and more with Alzheimer's. abstract

Counter: Carlson et al. (2024). 360-character alerts did not beat 90-character ones on compliance; shortness must not drop key content. abstract
G19
Use clear formatting and punctuation
B · XAINew, manual marking
 No all-caps or "!!!" in explanations (v2 says "Danger! Flood coming!"). Numbers written one consistent way ("3 in 10", "30%"). Style rules for the forecast card and the likelihood component. mixed evidence

Waugh et al. (2025), Weather, Climate & Society. Emphasising key text in a warning (bold or caps) aids attention and memory. Supports selective emphasis, not all-caps blocks. abstract

Klinke et al. (2024). All-uppercase text slows reading and hurts readability of long headlines. abstract

Counter: Arditi & Cho (2007), Vision Research. Uppercase was as legible or more at small sizes. abstract
G21
Provide summary of long documents
Need: Get the gist first
B · XAI
Not marked in Excel
Research Approach v2
 Open with a one-sentence bottom line: what, where, when, what to do The forecast card and the push notification both start with the same bottom-line sentence; reasons and evidence sit behind "Why?" and "How sure?"

Schwartz, Woloshin & Welch (2009), Ann Intern Med. Replacing a long summary with a one-table facts box raised correct understanding of benefit from 9% to 72% (two RCTs). abstract

Peixoto et al. (2025), preprint. Users said simplified LIME/SHAP explanations and an overview gave better quick understanding (n=3). full text

Nathe & Krakow (2019): patient understanding fell as page count rose. abstract

No study compares bottom line first vs later.
G22
Separate each instruction
B · XAINew, manual marking
 The action part of the explanation ("get your go-bag ready") is a numbered list, one step per line. Action list with a checkbox per step under the forecast card, fed by the CAP instruction field. mixed evidence

Desaulniers (1987), HFS Proc. Outline-format warnings were read and followed more than paragraph warnings. abstract

Morrow et al. (1998), Exp. Aging Res. List instructions were understood and recalled better and reduced age and working-memory differences. abstract

Counter: Frantz (1993), J. Safety Research. Prose vs numbered list did not change label effectiveness. abstract
G25
Explain implied content
Need: Understand what the prediction means
B · XAIResearch Approach v2
 Explain what a risk category means in concrete consequences, not the label alone Each app state (Safe, Forecast, Warning, Danger) stores a consequence line shown under its badge, e.g. "Water may come into houses near the river" mixed evidence

Weyrich et al. (2018), Weather, Climate & Society. Impact information plus recommendations improved understanding of the warning and intended response. abstract

Potter et al. (2018), IJDRR. Impact-based warnings increased understanding of potential impacts (n=1,364). abstract

Counter: Potter et al. found understanding did not raise action; Choi et al. (2025): impact-based heat warnings did not change intentions. abstract
G26
Alternatives for numerical concepts
Need: Understand likelihood without numeracy
B · XAIResearch Approach v2
 Word + picture (e.g. 3 of 10 houses), exact number on request One likelihood component renders the profile's form: word card, icon array of 10 houses, "30%" on tap; reused on the card, the notification and the "How sure?" screen mixed evidence

Galesic et al. (2009), Health Psychology. Icon arrays "increased accuracy of both low- and high-numeracy people", including adults aged 62–77. abstract

Gigerenzer & Hoffrage (1995), Psychol Rev: frequency formats improved reasoning. McDowell & Jacobs (2017), meta-analysis: natural frequencies and visual aids help. abstract

Counter: Galesic et al.: risks shown as icon arrays seemed "less serious"; matters for warnings. abstract
G47
Don't rely on user calculation
Need: Not work things out from numbers
B · XAI
Not marked in Excel
Research Approach v2
 "In 2 days" becomes "Thursday morning"; the system compares forecasts for the user The app converts lead time to day and part of day, and computes the change from the previous forecast; the user never subtracts dates or compares percentages

Peters et al. (2007), Med Care Res Rev. Formats that "ease the cognitive burden" gave higher comprehension, most for low-numeracy users. abstract

Liu et al. (2019), Aging Med: calculation and orientation to time are among the most impaired items in MCI and dementia (n=4,487). Boyle et al. (2013): cognitive decline predicts lower numeracy. abstract
G50
State results of actions
Need: Know what it means for their actions
B · XAIResearch Approach v2
 State what is likely to happen if they stay vs leave Action card under the forecast gives one line for staying and one for leaving, linked to the CAP instruction field

Sutton, Fischer & Wood (2021), Weather, Climate & Society. Protective action guidance increased understanding and the ability to decide what to do. abstract

Fischer et al. (2023). Guidance in text and graphic increased understanding and deciding across hazards. abstract

These test what to do, not an explicit stay-vs-leave comparison.
G59
Info and Relationships (SC 1.3.1)
B · XAINew, manual marking
 The explanation's structure (bottom line, reason, how sure) is exposed to screen readers. The icon array needs a text equivalent. accessibilityRole="header" on each step; icon array labelled "3 of 10 houses filled".

Sharif et al. (2021), ASSETS. Screen-reader users were 61% less accurate and took 211% longer with online charts; they asked for text and table versions. abstract

Zong et al. (2022), Comput. Graph. Forum. Structured, described charts helped blind readers understand data. abstract

Evidence is for visual impairment, not cognitive impairment. WCAG 1.3.1 text not re-checked.
G73
Persistent visual context
Need: Know where and when it applies
B · XAI
Not marked in Excel
Research Approach v2
 Always show the prediction's area and time window A fixed header on every forecast and explanation screen: area name, time window and the "Synthetic data" label

Klockow-McClain et al. (2020), Ann Am Assoc Geogr. Being inside or outside the warning area and distance changed risk estimates (N=5,564). abstract

Yoon et al. (2024), JMIR: location was the only alert content linked to people leaving the area. Peters-Founshtein et al. (2023), preprint: impaired spatio-temporal orientation in MCI. abstract
G89
Warning before any change
Need: Not be surprised by a revised forecast
B · XAI
Not marked in Excel
Research Approach v2
 Announce and explain an upgrade or downgrade before the new value When a new forecast changes the level, a "The forecast has changed" banner and notification appear before the new value, with a one-line reason mixed evidence

Burgeno & Joslyn (2020). Inconsistency between successive forecasts reduced trust. Losee & Joslyn (2018): inconsistency lowered trust but increased protective action. abstract

Counter: Burgeno et al. (2023): inconsistency increased trust with probabilistic forecasts. Baseman et al. (2013): each extra alert per week cut recall of content by 41%. abstract

No study tests announcing a change before the new value.
G118
Multi-modal alert (audio + visual + haptic)
B · XAINew, manual marking
 A forecast change or upgrade is announced through more than one channel. Overlaps G128. Forecast-change notification uses text, a voice line and a short vibration.

Stephens et al. (2013), Hum. Commun. Res. Three redundant messages, one through a synchronous channel, gave the fastest grasp of urgency (N=1,318). abstract

Tannenbaum-Baruchi et al. (2026), Disability & Health J. Sound + vibration + light app alerts; authors call for multimodal systems. Maguire et al. (2025), IJDRS: warnings in "multiple formats and channels". abstract

Both disability studies are with Deaf and hard-of-hearing adults; none with cognitive impairment.
G119
CAP message structure
Need: Get likelihood, severity, urgency in a standard form
B · XAIResearch Approach v2
 CAP's certainty field (Observed / Likely / Possible / Unlikely) gives a standard word for likelihood The forecast data model carries CAP fields (certainty, severity, urgency, instruction); the certainty word drives the likelihood word on every screen mixed evidence

Taylor et al. (2023), Risk Analysis. Likelihood words in warnings changed judgments; "unlikely" lowered perceived risk and action even for severe impacts. abstract

Budescu et al. (2009), Psychological Science. Readings of probability words deviated from official meanings; pair words with numbers. abstract

Counter to bare CAP words: supports adding the picture or number. OASIS CAP 1.2 text not re-checked.
G120
Universal hazard symbols
B · XAINew, manual marking
 The hazard invariant always carries the standard flood pictogram, so the hazard is clear without reading. One fixed flood symbol on every forecast, explanation and notification screen.

Shinar et al. (2013), Transp. Res. F. Adding text to a symbol improves comprehension and speed, especially for unfamiliar signs. abstract

Shorr et al. (2009), HFES. Older adults understood ANSI warning symbols less accurately and more slowly. abstract

Boedeker et al. (2023), J Gerontol B. Symbols with text cut errors for people with Alzheimer's dementia. abstract
G4
One step per screen
C · Both
Not marked in Excel
Research Approach v2
 Stage the explanation: bottom line, reason, evidence, uncertainty "Why?" opens a stepper: bottom line, reason, evidence, how sure, one screen each with Back and Next mixed evidence

Mindlin et al. (2024), preprint. Lay users who explored the explanation one question at a time understood the model significantly better than with a static all-at-once report (n=107); recommends layering "while not overwhelming the users with all information at once". full text

Bahel, Sriram & Conati (2024). Explanations split over pages opened one at a time raised engagement, understanding and learning. full text

Counter: Mindlin et al. found no difference in self-rated understanding or confidence.
G11
Most important stands out
C · Both
Excel marked B
Research Approach v2
 Lead with conclusion and action; the "why" is secondary Bottom line and action button fill the top of the forecast card; "Why?" is a smaller secondary button below

Peters et al. (2007), Med Care Res Rev. "Less is more": formats that highlight the meaning of important information gave higher comprehension, most for low-numeracy readers. abstract

Bahel et al. (2024). Showing the explanation more prominently than the conclusion confused users; making the conclusion dominant fixed it (pilot). full text

Mathur et al. (2026), CHI. Older adults used AI explanations to calibrate urgency and decide on action. full text
G41
Avoid too much content
C · Both
Excel marked B
Research Approach v2
 Progressive disclosure; detail level is a profile attribute The profile's "detail on first view" setting decides how many explanation parts render; the rest open on tap

Peters et al. (2007). Less information, focused on meaning, improved comprehension. abstract

Lage et al. (2019), preprint. Explanation complexity reduced human interpretability; new concepts cost more than repetition. abstract

Waterschoot et al. (2025), UMAP. More detailed explanations did not increase understanding (RCT, n=271). Monnet et al. (2024): low cognitive load is a key need in dementia decision tools. abstract
G48
Provide human help
C · Both
Not marked in Excel
Research Approach v2
 "Ask someone to explain" as an explanation option An "Ask someone to explain" button on explanation screens calls or messages the saved helper or the emergency hotline mixed evidence

Álvarez-Aguado et al. (2026), J Intellect Disabil Res. Adults with intellectual disability "rejected AI as a substitute for human support"; AI should complement their support network. abstract

Wilson et al. (2026), JARID. People with intellectual disability valued Easy Read health information "and assistance in appraising it". abstract

Counter: Buell et al. (2019), JARID. Human mediation alone did not significantly improve understanding (RCT, n=60); simplified text + mediation did best post hoc. abstract
G54
Provide reminders
C · Both
Not marked in Excel
Research Approach v2
 Reminders re-explain the current prediction and flag changes Scheduled local notifications restate the current forecast before its time window and say if it has changed mixed evidence

Herdener et al. (2018), Human Factors. Earlier hurricane forecasts anchored judgments, and cognitive load impaired adjusting to new information; supports flagging changes. abstract

Hackett et al. (2025): smartphone prompts raised task completion in MCI and mild dementia (n=10). abstract

Mixed: Ghani et al. (2026), JMIR. A reminder app for MCI gave only modest quality-of-life gains (RCT, n=1,078). abstract
G57
Support simplification
C · Both
Not marked in Excel
Research Approach v2
 Simple mode = minimal explanation that still keeps the invariants Simple mode shows one card with the five invariants only; history, map and reasons are hidden mixed evidence

Lage et al. (2019). Simpler explanations were more interpretable. abstract

Peixoto et al. (2025), preprint. Simplified LIME/SHAP explanations were more comprehensible for non-visual users (n=3, preliminary). full text

Counter: Xuan et al. (2023), IJHCS. Highly comprehensible explanations were most often misread, with overconfident inferences (n=200). Simple mode must keep the key facts. abstract
G58
Personalised, persistent interface
C · Both
Not marked in Excel
Research Approach v2
 Explanation profile persists and stays editable The profile is stored on the device, loaded at start-up and edited in Settings under "How we explain"

Bahel, Sriram & Conati (2024). Explanations personalised to user traits significantly increased engagement, understanding and learning. full text

Tielman et al. (2024). "Adaptive XAI is the key to inclusive XAI" (position paper). full text

No source tests whether the profile should persist or be editable.
G67
Reading level
C · Both
Excel marked B
Research Approach v2
 Explanation meets a target reading level; fuller version as supplement Explanation templates are written to the target reading level; a "More detail" link opens the fuller version

Walpole et al. (2025), Risk Analysis. "Evacuation warning" was poorly understood in emergency alerts across experience levels. abstract

Mikhail et al. (2015), RCT. Grade-5 pamphlets were understood better than grade-10 (57.9% vs 48.3%), whatever the reader's literacy. abstract
G76
Realistic, non-childish imagery
C · BothNew, manual marking
 The landmark comparison uses a real photo of the local bridge marker, not a cartoon. Per-gauge landmark photos stored with the local reference levels (already a capability in Task 5).

Wilhelmi et al. (2024), IJDRR. Realistic images of flooded buildings helped residents understand storm-surge forecasts. abstract

Zannino et al. (2007), (2010). Photos beat line drawings for naming in Alzheimer's. abstract

The "non-childish" part is untested.
G77
Avoid metaphorical icons
C · BothNew, manual marking
 "How sure?" uses no dice or gauge icon; reasons use literal rain and river icons. Icon set for explanation screens limited to literal pictograms with a word label.

Davies et al. (1998), Applied Ergonomics. Safety pictograms were poorly understood, "particularly those which were abstract". abstract

Boedeker et al. (2023): concrete symbols with text helped people with Alzheimer's. Wu et al. (2022), IJERPH: older adults with MCI did best with realistic icons plus text. abstract
G79
Co-design with people with dementia
C · BothNew, manual marking
 Explanation profiles should later be co-designed with users and carers. Not buildable in this paper (no user study). Fits the limitations and future-work section.

Tielman et al. (2024). People with cognitive disabilities "should be involved throughout the development process of XAI". full text

Atf & Lewis (2025). Recommends user-centred XAI "through participatory design". abstract

Strong evidence, but it is a method, not a UI rule.
G80
Caregiver companion view
C · Both
Not marked in Excel
Research Approach v2
 Carer may see more detail; same prediction and invariants The carer's view receives the same forecast with the exact figure and reasons, and shows which profile the user has

Mathur et al. (2026), CHI. AI explanations gave family members insight into older adults' daily lives; older adults imagined explanations shared with family. full text

Miller, Whitlatch & Lyons (2016), Dementia. Decisions are commonly shared between people with dementia and family carers (36 studies). abstract
G81
Use familiar, real-world metaphors only
C · BothNew, manual marking
 Likelihood as houses (icon array), not fan charts, error bars or probability curves. Only icon arrays and the landmark picture are used to show uncertainty and level.

Galesic et al. (2009), Health Psychology. Icon arrays "increased accuracy of both low- and high-numeracy people", incl. adults aged 62–77. abstract

Spiegelhalter et al. (2011), Science. Probabilities are "notoriously difficult to communicate" to lay audiences; graphic effectiveness depends on numeracy. abstract

Ruginski et al. (2015), CogSci. Non-experts misread hurricane-cone uncertainty displays. abstract
G88
Literal language with visual support
C · Both
Not marked in Excel
Research Approach v2
 Pair each explanation element with a pictogram or photo Every explanation line has a pictogram from one fixed set (rain, river rising, house, go-bag) mixed evidence

Fischer et al. (2023), Weather, Climate & Society. Warning messages with protective-action guidance in both text and graphic increased understanding, belief and deciding. abstract

Houts et al. (2006), Patient Educ Couns. Pictures linked to text "markedly increase attention to and recall" of health information; low-literacy readers benefit most. abstract

Counter: Hurtado et al. (2014), J Intellect Disabil Res. Text + pictures did no better than pictures only in an Easy Read leaflet. abstract
G107
Caregiver-mediated configuration
C · Both
Excel marked B
Research Approach v2
 Carer can set the profile with the user's consent A carer-assisted setup path in onboarding, with a consent screen before the carer changes the profile; consent is stored

Mathur et al. (2026), CHI. Older adults saw AI explanations as shared with family and wanted to configure which explanations are shareable and at what detail. full text

Largent et al. (2023), J Am Geriatr Soc: supported decision-making through a trusted supporter for people with MCI or mild dementia. Sinclair et al. (2019): families endorse it as reliance on others grows. abstract
G111
Calm colours for non-urgent states
C · Both
Not marked in Excel
Research Approach v2
 Colour follows risk category; a "possible" flood in 2 days is not red The Forecast state uses a calm v2 colour token; red is kept for Danger and live alerts mixed evidence

Chapanis (1994), Ergonomics. White, yellow, orange and red were read as successively greater hazard. abstract

Neussner (2021), IJDRR. Inconsistent colour codes in hazard warnings compromise understanding; recommends consistent yellow/orange/red levels. abstract

Counter: Leonard (1999) and Mayhorn et al. (2017). Colour–hazard pairings are not always recognised; orange and yellow can read as dangerous as red. abstract
G114
Time-boxed tasks with countdowns
C · Both
Not marked in Excel
Research Approach v2
 Time-to-event as a concrete day or countdown with its window A time chip reads "Thursday morning (about 2 days)"; preparation steps carry time estimates

Dallo et al. (2022), IJDRR. Hazard overviews with time and action indications significantly improved understanding of whether to act now (N=601). abstract

Marx et al. (2022), JAACAP: meta-analysis of broad timing deficits in ADHD. Zheng et al. (2022): less accurate time perception in ADHD. abstract
G117
Definition tooltips
C · Both
Excel marked B
Research Approach v2
 Tap-to-define any technical term left in the explanation Terms such as "danger level" or "upstream" are tappable and show a one-line definition mixed evidence

Bullock et al. (2019), Public Underst. Sci.. Jargon "impairs people's ability to process scientific information" (N=650). abstract

Counter: Shulman et al. (2020), J Lang Soc Psychol. Jargon disrupted processing "even when definitions for the jargon terms are provided"; removing jargon beats defining it. abstract

Tap-to-define tooltips themselves are untested.
G121
Local-language Easy Read track
C · Both
Excel marked B
Research Approach v2
 Easy Read explanation in Sinhala, Tamil, English Every explanation template exists in Sinhala, Tamil and English Easy Read; the language comes from the profile

Trujillo-Falcón et al. (2022), BAMS. Significant language inequities in understanding translated watch/warning terms; translate meaning, not words. abstract

Krocak et al. (2025), BAMS. Spanish speakers were less likely to receive, understand and respond to tornado warnings (N=14,341). abstract

Sri Lanka context: Chandrasekara (2026) reports language gaps in early warning during Cyclone Ditwah (observational).
G124
One decision at a time under stress
C · Both
Excel marked B
Research Approach v2
 Action-only while an alert is live; the "why" is deferred When the forecast becomes a live alert (Layer 3), every profile shows hazard, action and a call button only; "Why?" returns when the alert ends mixed evidence

Mathur et al. (2026), CHI. For emergency alerts, older adults wanted "direct, task-focused and concise explanations"; elaboration added cognitive load under imagined stress. full text

Starcke & Brand (2012): stress affects decision making. abstract

Counter: Joslyn & LeClerc (2012), J Exp Psychol Appl: advice alone did not improve decisions, advice + uncertainty did. Wood et al. (2018): longer warnings cut information-seeking delay. Keep "Why?" one tap away. abstract
G125
Pre-rehearsed action plan
C · Both
Not marked in Excel
Research Approach v2
 Teach what "Possible" and "Likely" mean in calm time A practice mode in calm time shows a sample "Possible" and "Likely" forecast and what to do for each

Budescu et al. (2009), Psychological Science. Readings of probability words deviated from the official meanings "even when the respondents had access to these guidelines"; pair words with numbers. abstract

Wakui et al. (2017): caregivers of people with dementia were 36% less likely to have an evacuation plan. abstract

No study shows that rehearsing warning terms in calm time improves later understanding.
G128
Multiple means of representation
C · Both
Not marked in Excel
Research Approach v2
 Same explanation as text, audio, symbols; modality is a profile attribute Each explanation is available as text, read-aloud audio and pictograms; the profile picks the default modality

Szymanski et al. (2021), IUI. Lay users preferred visual explanations but did worse with them; hybrid visual + text explanations improved understanding. abstract

Tait et al. (2012), Am J Med. Understanding was greater when the risk format matched the person's preferred format. abstract

Nuance: Delaunay et al. (2025), PACM HCI. Graphical vs text mainly changed confidence, not comprehension. abstract

Indirect 9 guidelines

G15
Use simple tense and voice
B · XAINew, manual marking
 "Flooding is possible here", not "A flood event has been forecast with moderate certainty". All explanation templates use active voice and present or simple future tense; checked in the screen spec. mixed evidence

Ferreira (2003), Cognitive Psychology. Passives "are frequently and systematically misinterpreted". abstract

Sung et al. (2020), JSLHR. People with MCI had more difficulty with passive than active sentences. abstract

Counter: Grossman & White-Devine (1998). Voice did not affect comprehension in Alzheimer's. No source found for tense. abstract
G17
Use literal language
B · XAINew, manual marking
 No metaphors for risk or confidence ("keep an eye on the river", "a perfect storm"). Fixed literal templates in Layer 2; the invariants check also rejects AI rewording with idioms.

Kalandadze et al. (2018), Autism. Meta-analysis: poorer figurative-language comprehension in autism (g = −0.57); not significant when matched on language ability. abstract

Morsanyi et al. (2021). Medium accuracy disadvantage for idioms in autism. abstract

No source on metaphor in AI explanations or warnings.
G38
Familiar metrics and units
Need: Relate quantities to known references
B · XAIResearch Approach v2
 River level as "knee-deep" or against a local landmark; local weekday and time River level drawn against the gauge's local landmark (bridge mark) from stored reference levels; times in Sri Lanka time with the weekday in Sinhala, Tamil or English

Giambastiani et al. (2024), preprint. Familiar-object scale pictures gave significantly better comprehension than text analogies alone (n=26). abstract

Chen et al. (2024), CHI. Analogies to familiar objects were rated as making data scale easier to grasp (self-rated). full text

No flood-depth or landmark study found. Words alone ("knee-deep") may be weaker than a picture.
G23
Use white spacing
C · BothNew, manual marking
 Space between explanation steps so each reads as its own unit. 1.5 line height and fixed gaps between steps on explanation screens.

Rello et al. (2015), UAIS. Larger text and character spacing sped reading for people with and without dyslexia. Reading speed, not comprehension. abstract
G24
Foreground not obscured by background
C · BothNew, manual marking
 No explanation text over the river photo or the icon array. Text sits beside or under images, never on top.

Mitzner et al. (2006), Human Factors. Low contrast slowed older readers, but comprehension was not significantly affected. abstract

Labarta et al. (2025), preprint. Legibility of XAI heatmaps changed how understandable they were (N=455). abstract
G27
Controls/content do not move unexpectedly
C · BothNew, manual marking
 A revised forecast must not reflow the card under the user's finger; the change is announced first (G89). Forecast card keeps fixed slots; new values arrive behind a "The forecast has changed" banner.

Bergmann et al. (2016). Older adults detected fewer changes, even in easy tasks. Veiel et al. (2006): older adults slower to find changes. abstract

Simons et al. (2000), Perception. Large gradual changes go unnoticed; supports announcing a revision. abstract
G49
Alternative content for complex info
C · Both
Excel marked B
Research Approach v2
